Part 1

How the course runs

01

The idea of the course

You only learn from your mistakes

In plain words

Most courses explain everything first and let you practise afterwards. This one flips it: you get a problem first, you try, you get stuck, and then the lecture fills exactly the gap you just discovered. It can feel frustrating. That is the point, and it is also why you should ask for help early.

How it works
  • Goal: learn to derive information from programs using different techniques, by building analyses yourself.
  • The challenge: do as well as possible on the JPAMB benchmark, a suite of small Java methods whose possible outcomes your analyses must predict.
  • Teaching philosophy: "You only learn from your mistakes." Material sticks when it fills a gap you found by failing at a task, so the course is practical and hands-on.
  • Didactic contract: 7.5 ECTS over 13 weeks + exam week at 28 hours per ECTS point is about 14 hours per week, only 4 of them in class. Good programming experience is expected.
  • Attendance is not mandatory, but the lectures are the primary way the material is conveyed; it is very hard to pass without engaging.
  • Feedback is welcome by mail or an anonymous form; the course is still evolving.
Key ideaBudget about 10 hours a week outside class for building analyses. Building things is where the learning happens.
02

A lecture day and the groups

Feedback first, then lecture, then lab

In plain words

Every Monday afternoon starts with talking to a few classmates about what went well and what did not, then the whole class sorts out the biggest questions, then there is a lecture, and the rest of the afternoon you build things with TAs around.

How it works
TimeWhat happens
13:00Peer feedback in your feedback group: progress, what was easy or hard, and last lecture's questions.
13:20Communal feedback: the class summarises and resolves the biggest questions.
13:30Lecture: interactive, about the problem of the day.
15:00Lab work with the TAs.
  • Every lecture ends with 2 or 3 questions about program analysis. You discuss them in your feedback group the week after, and they may be asked at the oral exam. Each lecture page on this site has them, with model answers, under "Exam questions".
  • Feedback group: about 4 random students, to hear alternative solutions; you sit together at the start of every class.
  • Project group: 3 to 5 students who do the final project and defend it together. Fixed when the proposal is handed in, and it may not overlap with your feedback group.
  • Shepherds: each project group gets a permanent TA who helps with the project.
  • Mondays 13 to 17, room 358-060A. Teacher: Christian Gram Kalhauge; TAs: Oskar, Alessandro and Emil.

Part 2

The project and the exam

04

Choosing a project and the proposal

Two pages that set your ambition

In plain words

Pick a program analysis problem you find interesting, typically some JPAMB cases you want to do better on, and promise to try several solutions and compare them. The proposal is basically the introduction of your future paper, and it must convince the teachers you know what you are doing.

How it works
  • The topic must centre on program analysis and show your understanding of the course. Common approach: choose benchmark cases to improve on, implement several solutions, and evaluate them on speed and precision. Custom projects are possible after talking to the teacher.
  • The proposal is about 2 pages, ACM small format, and corresponds roughly to the introduction of the final paper.
  • It is graded 1 to 4 on each of six criteria (the CGIE model plus plan and presentation):
    • Context: what is the problem?
    • Gap: why do current techniques not work?
    • Innovation: what is your proposed solution?
    • Evaluation: when have you solved the problem?
    • Plan: what is the plan?
    • Presentation: is it formatted correctly and presented nicely?
  • You need at least 12 points and no criterion at 1. A rejected proposal must be resubmitted, even after the deadline. Proposals are graded as they come in.
  • Append the table of technical contributions (see topic 06), including the maximal grade of each student.
Formally

Rule Accepting a proposal

With scores $s_1, \ldots, s_6 \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ for the six criteria, $$\text{accepted} \iff \sum_{i=1}^{6} s_i \geq 12 \;\wedge\; \forall i.\; s_i \neq 1.$$

"Accepted exactly when the six scores add up to at least 12 and none of them is a 1."

05

How you are assessed

Paper, overview sheet, video, oral defence

In plain words

Your grade is one overall judgement of what your group wrote, what each of you contributed, and how well you can explain it when asked. Using the course's techniques, doing them well, and backing up your claims with experiments is what counts.

How it works
  • The final grade is an overall assessment of: the paper (per project group), the individualised project overview sheet, and the video presentation and oral defence (done in project groups).
  • It is measured against the course's learning objectives, with special weight on:
    1. using the content of the course (shown by your technical contributions),
    2. a well-done project: easy to read, techniques warranted by the problem, claims supported by evaluation,
    3. for extra points: an interesting problem and an innovative solution.
  • Proceedings: after the exam, papers are collected (voluntarily) into proceedings shared with participants and future students, and a best paper is awarded each semester.
06

Technical contributions and the suggested grade

Points, diversity, and the maximal grade per student

In plain words

The course keeps a menu of things worth doing in a project, each with a point value. Your group adds up what it did, splits the points between its members, and every student's points (and how many different things they worked on) suggest the best grade they can aim for. It is advice, not a promise.

How it works

The overview sheet (one A4 page) holds name, student number, feedback group and a photo of each member, plus a table with one row per contribution: the contribution, the points it is worth, and how those points are split between the students.

CodeContributionFormulaMax
GradePointsDiversity

Formally

Rule Points of a contribution

A contribution $c$ with formula $f_c$ (e.g. "5 per analysis") and maximum $m_c$, done $n$ times, is worth $$\mathrm{pts}(c) = \min\big(m_c,\; f_c(n)\big),$$ and the group splits it: $\sum_{s} \mathrm{pts}_s(c) \leq \mathrm{pts}(c)$ over the students $s$.

Rule Suggested maximal grade

For a student $s$ let $P_s = \sum_c \mathrm{pts}_s(c)$ and $D_s = |\{c \mid \mathrm{pts}_s(c) > 0\}|$. With the table rows $(g, p_g, d_g)$, $$\mathrm{grade}(s) = \max\{\, g \mid P_s \geq p_g \wedge D_s \geq d_g \,\}$$ (and no suggested passing grade if no row applies).

"The highest grade whose point threshold and diversity threshold the student both meets."

CarefulThe note stresses that technical contributions are an advisory grade that sets expectations. At the exam your grade can be higher or lower depending on the project as a whole. The list is also incomplete and open to suggestions by mail.
07

What the paper must contain

At most 10 pages, ACM acmsmall

In plain words

The paper is a short research paper: here is a problem, here is our idea, here is the theory behind it, here is the proof that it works, here is how it compares with other ideas. Ten pages, a fixed template, and an appendix that does not count.

How it works
SectionPagesMust contain
Abstracta description of the paper
Introduction1 to 2motivation and problem (use examples); the problem as a research question; a short description of the solution; a list of contributions with forward references
Example1 to 2an example illustrating your approach
Approach and theory2 to 3a formal description of the approach; a walkthrough of the theory needed; any theoretical guarantees
Evaluation2 to 3an empirical evaluation, compared against at least one other technique (or variations of yours); analysis of the results; threats to validity
Related work1 to 2alternative techniques for the same problem, and why the other techniques from the lectures (and literature) were not used
Conclusion0 to 1/2a short recap
Appendixnot counteddata availability statement, AI-use statement, references
  • At most 10 pages excluding the appendix, ACM format in acmsmall mode.
  • Before writing, the note recommends Simon Peyton Jones' talk on writing a great research paper (summarised in Part 4).
08

Video and oral defence

Five minutes of video, then questions

In plain words

You record a short talk about your project before the exam. At the exam it is played first, and then the examiners ask each of you questions about your own contributions and about the course.

How it works
  • Video: handed in the week of the exam; formatted like a normal oral presentation, at most 5 minutes. Use slides (record with e.g. OBS), include examples and diagrams that can guide the oral exam, and number your slides so the examiners can navigate.
  • Oral defence: in project groups, about 30 minutes per group (plan still being worked out): the video first, ending with individual questioning.
  • Questions cover your technical contributions and the course material in general, especially the lecture questions (the "Exam questions" sections on this site).
Part 3

Writing a good paper

09

Write coherently

Remember the reader

In plain words

Your reader is busy and reading your paper is not their priority. Every confusing sentence spends a bit of their patience. Make each paragraph say one thing, let each sentence pick up where the last one left off, and say who did what.

How it works
  • One point per paragraph. State the point in the first sentence; the rest of the paragraph supports it. Skimmers then still get your argument.
  • Flow, old to new. Start each sentence with something the reader already knows and end with the new information. Flow breaks when a sentence opens with something new and unrelated.

    No flow: Program analysis is one of the most fun disciplines in computer science. After taking this course, we hope that the students will feel this as well. Concolic execution is a great example of why people should know about program analysis. Sadly, it is not well known.

    Flow: ... we hope that the students will feel that as well. One example of a program analysis that might entice students to take and enjoy the course, is concolic execution. Sadly, concolic execution is not well known.

  • Active voice. Let a subject do every action; when you did something, write "we".

    Don't: A thorough investigation of the causes of the inaccuracies in the analysis was done, and no clear cause were found.
    Do: We did a thorough investigation of the inaccuracies in the analysis, and we found no clear cause.

    Don't: Contained in this paper, are many truths!
    Do: This paper contains many truths!

  • Read it aloud. You will hear quickly whether your sentences make sense.
  • Orwell's six rules: (1) never use a metaphor or figure of speech you are used to seeing in print; (2) never use a long word where a short one will do; (3) if you can cut a word, cut it; (4) never use the passive where you can use the active; (5) never use a foreign phrase, scientific word or jargon if there is an everyday English equivalent; (6) break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.
10

Structure and title

Fewer readers at every section

In plain words

Think of the paper as a funnel. Thousands see the title, far fewer read the abstract, a hundred read the introduction, and only a handful read the technical details. So the parts most people read deserve the most care.

How it works
Title1 sentence · 10,000 readers
Abstract1 to 2 paragraphs · 1,000 readers
Introduction1 to 2 pages · 100 readers
Key idea2 to 3 pages · 50 readers
Technical meat3 to 5 pages · 10 readers
Related work1 to 2 pages · 100 readers
Conclusion0 to 1/2 page · 100 readers
  • The title is the most important part: it invites people in. Use keywords people recognise and say what the method is.
  • Bad: "Project 10"; "Group 10" (your group is in the author list); "Program Analysis" (says nothing); "Testcase Prediction" (ambiguous, method unclear).
  • Good: "Test Case Selection using Static Call-Graph Analysis"; "Testing Static Analysis using LLM-Synthesized Code"; "Confirming Static Analysis Results using Directed Dynamic Analysis".
11

Abstract and introduction: the CGIE model

Context, Gap, Innovation, Evaluation

In plain words

An abstract tells a tiny story: here is a world the reader cares about, here is what is broken in it, here is our idea, and here is the evidence that it works. After the introduction the examiner already knows the best grade you can get; the rest of the paper proves you deserve it. So write the introduction first, even before the work is finished.

How it works
  • Context: what the reader needs to know to be motivated about the problem.
  • Gap: why current approaches do not solve it completely (cite some prior work here, not all of it).
  • Innovation: your key idea and how it sets you apart, including how you perform on the problem.
  • Evaluation: how you have shown that it works.

(Context) The regression test-suites of big software projects can contain thousands of tests. Running all of these tests can take multiple days. Selecting which tests to run is therefore crucial to maintain developer productivity. (Gap) Currently, developers will manually select which tests to run after a change. This is inefficient and error-prone.

(Innovation) In this paper, we use syntactic static analysis and information logged in an initial run of the test-suite, to predict which test might have changed. Our key insight is ... (Evaluation) In a case study, we ran our approach on Maven, which has 8323 tests. Over the last 300 commits we were able to correctly predict 98% of the changed tests, and were able to on average save 23.2 min of test time per commit.

The introduction is an expanded abstract that also:

  • references prior work in the gap (the rest goes to related work),
  • uses the extra space for a small motivating example that anchors the reader,
  • includes a process diagram of your idea,
  • ends with a bullet list of contributions with forward references. Don't let your reader guess what you did.
12

Key idea and technical meat

Theory, implementation, evaluation, threats to validity

In plain words

First show your idea on a small example that anyone can follow. Then the serious part: why it is correct, how you built it (briefly), and experiments that show it beats the alternative, together with an honest list of what could make your experiment misleading.

How it works
  • Key idea: illustrate a (possibly limited) version of your approach with one or more examples, readable by people outside the field. The note points to sections 2 and 3 of Kalhauge and Palsberg (2019), Binary reduction of dependency graphs, as a good example.
  • Theory: argue with math that the approach is sound, as rigorously as your work allows.
  • Implementation: usually uninteresting; a paragraph or two on how it differs from the theory.
  • Evaluation: the reason for the implementation. Start with a summary and a list of sub-research questions that each answer part of the main research question, then fill in one section per question. Check that you answered the research question (or change it), analysed the results statistically (do they vary?), and thought about threats to validity (or reduced your scope).
  • Threats to validity: internal (measurement errors in your experiment, e.g. a noisy laptop, corrected by repeated measurements) and external (your scope limits what you may generalise, e.g. a small set of Java benchmarks, or implementations written by you).

Example opening of an evaluation: "In this section, we answer our main research question: Is static analysis better, in terms of speed and accuracy, than dynamic at detecting information flow bugs? ... We then asked two questions: How fast are the analyses? ... Can we tune the dynamic analysis? ... While our experiments are limited by the scope (see Section 6.5), we conclude that dynamic analysis is faster, but also more imprecise than static analysis, in detecting information flow bugs."

13

Related work and conclusion

Explain why the alternatives fall short

In plain words

Related work is not a reading list. For each alternative, say why it does not solve your problem as well as your approach does. The conclusion is just a short recap.

How it works
  • Compare with academic papers (search Google Scholar), known tools, or techniques from the course.
  • Don't: "There exist other techniques that also solve our problem, like fuzz testing and symbolic execution."
  • Do: "We could also have used fuzz testing to solve our problem, however it does have two major drawbacks compared to our technique. Firstly, it requires a ... The same limitations apply to symbolic execution."
  • The paper must explain why other techniques from the lectures were not used, so related work is also where you show you know the whole course.
